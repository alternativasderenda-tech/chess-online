10 Chess Openings for Beginners

Lesson 11 of 14Beginner⏱ 6 min readUpdated October 26, 2026

The best chess openings for beginners are the ones that follow the basic principles — control the center, develop your pieces and castle early — without demanding a lot of memorized theory. This guide covers 10: as White, the Italian Game, the Spanish (Ruy Lopez), the Queen's Gambit and the English; as Black, the Sicilian, the French, the Caro-Kann, the Slav and the Pirc/Modern; plus the London System, a system opening. To start, three are enough: one as White, one against 1.e4 and one against 1.d4.

Before you choose: the principles

If you've read the opening principles, you already have the essentials: control the center, develop your knights and bishops, castle early, don't move the same piece over and over, and don't bring your queen out too early. Every opening below follows those ideas, each in its own way. Pick by style, not by fame.

Openings to play as White

1. Italian Game

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4

The most classic opening there is, and the number-one recommendation for beginners. The ideas are simple and direct: occupy the center, develop your minor pieces, and target your opponent's weak spot (the f7 square).

Why it's good: quick to learn, a natural attack, and it teaches the principles in practice.

2. Spanish Opening (Ruy Lopez)

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5

Considered the most respected opening in chess history. The bishop on b5 pressures the knight on c6, which defends the central pawn. The pressure is strategic and long-lasting.

Why it's good: it teaches a lot about position, but it has long variations — start with the Italian and switch later.

3. Queen's Gambit

1.d4 d5 2.c4

It's a "gambit" because it offers a pawn, but that pawn is hard to hold on to. Black almost always declines it or accepts it only temporarily. Excellent for players who prefer maneuvering and piling up small advantages to attacking the king directly.

Why it's good: solid positions, clear plans, and a strong foundation for understanding strategy.

4. English Opening

1.c4

Instead of pushing the king's pawn, White starts with the c-pawn, on the queenside. It's flexible and leads to positions that beginner opponents have explored less.

Why it's good: it sidesteps heavy theory and takes your opponent out of their memorized lines ("the book").

Defenses to play as Black

5. Sicilian Defense

1.e4 c5

The most aggressive defense against 1.e4 and the most popular at the professional level. Black attacks the center from the queenside instead of mirroring White's pawn. Asymmetrical, dynamic positions.

Heads up: it has a LOT of theory. As a beginner, start with lower-theory systems such as the Kan (2.Nf3 e6 and ...a6) or the Accelerated Dragon (2.Nf3 Nc6 and ...g6), and leave the Najdorf for later.

6. French Defense

1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5

Black builds a pawn chain (pawns protecting each other diagonally, like e6 and d5) and closes the center. Strategic and patient. Excellent for players who don't enjoy wild tactical games.

Why it's good: clear plans (hitting White's pawn chain with …c5 and …f6) and few surprises.

7. Caro-Kann Defense

1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5

Similar to the French, but with a more active c8 bishop. Less theory, more simplicity. World champions such as Botvinnik, Petrosian and Karpov played it often.

Why it's good: a sturdy defense, easy to learn, ideal for beginners playing Black against 1.e4.

8. Slav Defense

1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6

The classic answer to the Queen's Gambit. Black defends the d5 pawn without locking the c8 bishop behind an e6 pawn, the problem with the traditional Queen's Gambit Declined (1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6).

Why it's good: solid, easy to play, and it leads to positions you can understand.

9. Pirc / Modern Defense

1.e4 d6 2.d4 Nf6 3.Nc3 g6 (Pirc) or 1.e4 g6 (Modern)

Black lets White occupy the center and attacks it later with pieces and pawns (the "hypermodern" school). Flexible positions that demand understanding rather than rote memorization.

Why it's good: it saves on memorization. You play "your own way".

System openings (the easiest to learn)

10. London System

1.d4 d5 2.Nf3 Nf6 3.Bf4

The famous "lazy opening". You play almost the same moves regardless of what your opponent does: d4, Nf3, Bf4, e3, Bd3, c3, Nbd2 and O-O (kingside castling). Within about 10 moves you have your whole structure set up.

Why it's good: practically zero memorization, a universal system, and time to think while you set up. Ideal for beginners.

How to choose your opening

Don't try to memorize them all. Pick around three openings and play all your games with them until you master them. A practical suggestion for beginners:

AggressiveItalian GameSicilianSlav
Positional or want to study lessLondon SystemCaro-KannSlav
Want varietyEnglishPircPirc
Against 1.d4, the defenses in this guide are more solid than aggressive: even if you like to attack, the Slav is the simplest choice to start with.

💡 Honest tip: don't spend your first week memorizing moves. Spend your first week playing games with the Italian Game as White and the Caro-Kann as Black. Then review those games to see where you went wrong. You'll learn far more this way than from reading a manual.

Common opening mistakes

  • Bringing the queen out too early. She gets chased around by minor pieces and you waste time retreating.
  • Attacking before developing. A lone piece doesn't take down an army. Develop first, attack later.
  • Capturing pawns for no reason. Often the pawn is "poisoned": you win it, but lose time and position.
  • Forgetting to castle. A king stuck in the center is an invitation to checkmate. Castle before move 10.
  • Memorizing without understanding. When your opponent leaves the book (and they will), you're left with no direction.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best chess opening for beginners?

There isn't just one. As White, the Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4) is one of the most common recommendations, because it applies the principles naturally; as Black, the Caro-Kann (1.e4 c6) is solid and needs little theory.

How many openings does a beginner need?

Three is enough: one to play as White, one answer to 1.e4 and one to 1.d4. Playing the same ones every game teaches you more than knowing a little of many.

Do I need to memorize opening moves?

No. The first moves of every opening come from the opening principles, and they tell you what to do when your opponent leaves the familiar path. Memorizing long lines only pays off later, once you have a repertoire.
