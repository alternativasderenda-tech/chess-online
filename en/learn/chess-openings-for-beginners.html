10 Chess Openings for Beginners

📅 Published on May 11, 2026 • ⏱ 9 min read

You don't need to memorize 20 moves of every opening. You need to understand the principles and keep, at most, three openings you're comfortable with in your repertoire. This guide walks through the 10 most important openings for a beginner and helps you decide which ones to adopt.

In this guide

  1. The principles that matter more than memorizing moves
  2. Openings to play as White
  3. Defenses to play as Black
  4. System openings (easy to learn)

The principles that matter more than memorizing moves

Almost every beginner makes the same mistake: they try to memorize long sequences of famous openings, assume the game is over the moment they lose the thread, and get discouraged. You don't need any of that. The truth is that the principles cover 95% of opening situations:

  • Control the center. The four central squares (d4, d5, e4, e5) are the most valuable on the board. Pieces in the center have more mobility.
  • Develop your minor pieces first. Knights before bishops. In most openings, knights go to c3, f3, c6, and f6.
  • Castle as early as possible. Ideally by move ten. A safe king means an attack is possible.
  • Don't move the same piece twice in the opening. Unless you have a good reason (capturing an important piece, dodging an attack).
  • Don't bring your queen out early. She becomes an easy target for minor pieces and you waste time retreating.

If you follow these five principles, you'll get playable positions in any opening, even without memorizing variations.

Openings to play as White

1. Italian Game

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4

The most classic opening there is, and the number-one recommendation for beginners. The ideas are simple and direct: occupy the center, develop your minor pieces, and target your opponent's weak spot (the f7 square).

Why it's good: quick to learn, a natural attack, and it teaches the principles in practice.

2. Spanish Opening (Ruy Lopez)

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5

Considered the most respected opening in chess history. The bishop on b5 pressures the knight on c6, which defends the central pawn. The pressure is strategic and long-lasting.

Why it's good: it teaches a lot about position, but it has long variations — start with the Italian and switch later.

3. Queen's Gambit

1.d4 d5 2.c4

It's a "gambit" because it offers a pawn, but that pawn is hard to hold on to. Black almost always declines it or accepts it only temporarily. Excellent for players who prefer positional play over direct attacks.

Why it's good: solid positions, clear plans, and a strong foundation for understanding strategy.

4. English Opening

1.c4

Instead of pushing the king's pawn, White starts with the queenside pawn on the flank. It's flexible and leads to positions that beginner opponents have explored less.

Why it's good: it sidesteps heavy theory and takes your opponent out of the book.

Defenses to play as Black

5. Sicilian Defense

1.e4 c5

The most aggressive defense against 1.e4 and the most popular at the professional level. Black attacks the center from the queenside instead of mirroring White's pawn. Asymmetrical, dynamic positions.

Heads up: it has a LOT of theory. As a beginner, it's worth starting with simpler variations like the Najdorf or the Accelerated Dragon.

6. French Defense

1.e4 e6 2.d4 d5

Black builds a solid pawn chain and closes the center. Strategic and patient. Excellent for players who don't enjoy wild tactical games.

Why it's good: clear plans (the f-pawn break) and few surprises.

7. Caro-Kann Defense

1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5

Similar to the French, but with a more active c8 bishop. Less theory, more simplicity. Karpov used it his entire life.

Why it's good: a sturdy defense, easy to learn, ideal for beginners playing Black against 1.e4.

8. Slav Defense

1.d4 d5 2.c4 c6

The classic answer to the Queen's Gambit. Black defends the central pawn without locking in the c8 bishop (the problem with the traditional Queen's Gambit Declined).

Why it's good: solid, easy to play, and it leads to positions you can understand.

9. Pirc / Modern Defense

1.e4 d6 2.d4 g6 (Pirc) or 1.e4 g6 (Modern)

Black hands over the center early and attacks it later. Hypermodern. Flexible positions that demand understanding rather than rote memorization.

Why it's good: it saves on memorization. You play "your own way".

System openings (the easiest to learn)

10. London System

1.d4 d5 2.Nf3 Nf6 3.Bf4

The famous "lazy opening". You play almost the same moves regardless of what your opponent does: d4, Nf3, Bf4, e3, Bd3, c3, Nbd2, O-O. Within about 10 moves you have your whole structure set up.

Why it's good: practically zero memorization, a universal system, and time to think while you set up. Ideal for beginners.

How to choose your opening

Don't try to memorize them all. Pick around three openings and play all your games with them until you master them. A practical suggestion for beginners:

Common opening mistakes

  • Bringing the queen out too early. She gets chased around by minor pieces and you waste time retreating.
  • Attacking before developing. A lone piece doesn't take down an army. Develop first, attack later.
  • Capturing pawns for no reason. Often the pawn is poisoned — you win material but lose time and position.
  • Forgetting to castle. A king stuck in the center is an invitation to checkmate. Castle before move 10.
  • Memorizing without understanding. When your opponent leaves the book (and they will), you're left with no direction.
