Italian Game: Main Lines, Plans and Traps

Intermediate⏱ 7 min readUpdated October 26, 2026

The Italian Game is the chess opening that starts with 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4: White develops the bishop to c4, where it eyes f7, the weakest square in Black's camp early in the game. It splits into two families: the direct approach, where White opens the center with d4 (the Giuoco Piano and the Evans Gambit), and the slow maneuvering approach with c3 and d3 (the Giuoco Pianissimo). If Black answers 3...Nf6, the game becomes the Two Knights Defense.

The main line and what each move does

1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4

The e4 pawn takes the center, the knight on f3 hits e5 and the knight on c6 defends it. With 3.Bc4, White develops the last kingside minor piece, gets ready to castle and points at f7, which only the king protects.

On move 3, Black picks the kind of game:

  • 3...Bc5 puts the bishop on the mirror square, aiming at f2. This is the Giuoco Piano ("quiet game" in Italian). It can turn into an open fight (4.c3 and 5.d4), a slow game (4.c3 and 5.d3) or a gambit (4.b4, the Evans Gambit).
  • 3...Nf6, the Two Knights Defense, counterattacks e4 and invites complications.
  • 3...Be7 is solid but passive. Not a mistake, though it hands White the initiative.

If you're still deciding whether the Italian is for you, 10 chess openings for beginners compares the options. This article is about the lines and the plans.

Giuoco Piano: 4.c3 and the fight for the center

White's classical plan is easy to state: c3 to support d4, then d4 to grab the center with tempo against the c5 bishop.

The main line goes 5...exd4 6.cxd4 Bb4+ 7.Bd2 Bxd2+ 8.Nbxd2 d5!. That 8...d5 is the standard reply: Black hits the center before White can consolidate e4 and d4. After 9.exd5 Nxd5 the position is considered balanced. White has an isolated pawn on d4 and active pieces, while Black tries to blockade and target that pawn.

What each side wants:

  • White: castle quickly, play Re1 and aim the pieces at the black king. The d4 pawn gives space but needs looking after.
  • Black: trade pieces (every trade takes steam out of White's attack), park a knight on d5, pressure d4.

Instead of 7.Bd2, 7.Nc3 gives up the e4 pawn for fast development and leads to very sharp play. Study it carefully before you adopt it.

Giuoco Pianissimo: the slow plan with d3

With 5.d3, White stops trying to open the center right away. The idea is to maneuver first and only play d4 once everything is ready. It is very popular among strong players because it avoids forcing lines and keeps plenty of pieces on the board.

Typical plans:

  • White: the Nbd2-f1-g3 maneuver brings the knight to the kingside. Then h3 (against ...Bg4 and ...Ng4) and sometimes a4, and only when everything is in place, d4.
  • Black: ...a6 and ...Ba7 keep the bishop safe from d4 or b4; ...h6 takes g5 away from White's pieces; ...Be6 offers to trade off White's b3 bishop.

Move order matters more than memory here. Once you understand why the knight heads for g3 and why Black's bishop drops back to a7, you can play these positions without memorizing lines.

Evans Gambit: 4.b4

The Evans Gambit offers the b4 pawn to gain time. If the bishop takes, c3 comes with tempo and d4 builds the center.

The most common line is 4...Bxb4 5.c3 Ba5 6.d4. White is a pawn down but gets the center and a lead in development. Black has to give material back at the right moment or trade pieces to ease the pressure. If you don't want that kind of fight, decline with 4...Bb6, the Evans Gambit Declined.

Two Knights Defense: 3...Nf6

With 3...Nf6, Black attacks e4 and invites White's most aggressive try: 4.Ng5, bringing a second piece to bear on f7.

The main defense is 4...d5 5.exd5 Na5!. The knight hits the bishop and Black gives up a pawn for development: 6.Bb5+ c6 7.dxc6 bxc6 8.Be2 h6 9.Nf3 e4 10.Ne5. White is a pawn up; Black has active pieces and space.

There are other paths:

  • 4...Bc5, the Traxler Counterattack, ignores the threat and goes after f2. It is risky for both sides.
  • For White, 4.d3 or 4.d4 sidestep the sharp theory. After 4.d3 the game drifts back toward Pianissimo-style maneuvering.

Typical Italian Game traps

The Fried Liver: dragging the black king into the center

In the Two Knights, after 4...d5 5.exd5, the natural 5...Nxd5?! is dangerous. White can sacrifice the knight on f7 and pull Black's king into the middle of the board: this is the famous Fried Liver Attack. In practice Black has a miserable time defending, which is why theory recommends 5...Na5.

The Blackburne Shilling Gambit: when White falls for it

Not every Italian trap favors White. After 3...Nd4?!, Black leaves e5 undefended and 4.Nxe5? looks very tempting. But 4...Qg5! forks the e5 knight and the g2 pawn. If White stays greedy with 5.Nxf7??, then 5...Qxg2 6.Rf1 Qxe4+ 7.Be2 and Black has mate.

💡 The right answer to 3...Nd4: don't take on e5. Play 4.Nxd4 exd4 and castle, or simply 4.O-O. The move 3...Nd4 moves the same knight twice in the opening, and without the trap it just costs Black time.

Who should play the Italian Game

The Italian suits almost every style because you choose the tempo:

  • You like to attack: the Giuoco Piano with 5.d4, the Evans Gambit, and 4.Ng5 against the Two Knights.
  • You prefer maneuvering: the Giuoco Pianissimo with d3, which gives strategic positions in the spirit of the Ruy Lopez with less theory.
  • You're moving past the beginner stage: it's the natural bridge from opening principles to a real repertoire. The ideas (center, castling, pressure on f7) are the same ones you met in the Scholar's Mate, just applied with patience.

If you play Black and face 1.e4 a lot, also look at the Sicilian Defense, which avoids the Italian from move one.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between the Giuoco Piano and the Giuoco Pianissimo?

Both start from 3...Bc5. In the Giuoco Piano, White plays c3 and d4 to open the center quickly; in the Pianissimo, White plays d3 and maneuvers before opening the game.

Is the Italian better than the Ruy Lopez?

Neither is objectively better. The Ruy Lopez (3.Bb5) has more theory and long-term strategic pressure; the Italian is more direct and easier to learn.

What should I play against the Two Knights if I want to avoid theory?

Play 4.d3. You protect e4, avoid the forcing lines after 4.Ng5 and get a maneuvering game.

Is the Evans Gambit sound?

It's a respected gambit: White gets real compensation (time and the center) for the pawn. It does require you to know how to attack, because a badly handled attack leaves you with nothing but a missing pawn.
