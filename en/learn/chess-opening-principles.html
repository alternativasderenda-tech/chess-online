Chess Opening Principles for Beginners

Beginner⏱ 6 min readUpdated October 26, 2026

Opening principles are rules of thumb for the first moves of a game: control the center, develop your knights and bishops, castle early, and don't move the same piece over and over or bring your queen out too soon. Follow them and you'll reach the middlegame with your pieces ready for action and your king safe, without memorizing a single opening.

Why principles beat memorized moves

The opening is the first phase of the game, roughly the first 10 to 15 moves, in which both sides bring their pieces out from the starting position. There are hundreds of named openings, but memorizing sequences doesn't help if you don't know why each move is good. As soon as your opponent leaves the script, a player who only memorized is lost.

Principles work in every opening. They answer the question that matters on every move: does this move help my pieces get into the game?

1. Control the center

The center is the four squares d4, e4, d5 and e5. A piece in the center reaches more squares and can switch quickly to either side of the board. That's why the most popular first moves, 1.e4 and 1.d4, put a pawn in the center — and open lines for the queen and a bishop at the same time.

Controlling the center doesn't only mean occupying it: a knight on f3, for example, attacks e5 and d4 without standing on them.

2. Develop your pieces

Developing means moving a piece off its starting square to an active square. In the opening, the priority is the minor pieces: knights and bishops.

  • Knights before bishops, as a rule: a knight's best squares (f3, c3, f6, c6) are almost always the same, while a bishop can wait to see where it's most useful.
  • Knights toward the center, not the edge. A knight on a3 or h3 controls fewer squares.
  • One move, one new piece. Moving the same piece two or three times for no reason gives your opponent time to develop theirs.

3. Castle early

A king in the center is a target: when the central files open up, it's exposed. Castling takes the king out of the middle and brings a rook into play at the same time. To castle, you first need to develop the knight and bishop on that side to clear the way.

A good goal for beginners: castle within the first 10 moves. If you're unsure how castling works, see the special moves.

4. Don't bring your queen out too early

The queen is your most valuable piece (9 points). If she comes out early, your opponent attacks her with minor pieces, and with every attack they develop a piece and gain a tempo (a move), while you spend moves retreating.

That doesn't mean the queen may never move in the opening, only that she shouldn't go out on her own, ahead of the minor pieces.

5. Four more tips

  • Don't push too many pawns. Two central pawns are usually enough early on. Edge pawns (a, h) rarely help in the opening.
  • Be careful with the f-pawn. Moving it early opens a diagonal to your king (the Scholar's Mate targets exactly the f7 square).
  • Connect your rooks. Once your minor pieces are out, you've castled and your queen has stepped off the back rank, your rooks see each other. That's a good sign the opening is over.
  • Don't grab material at the cost of development. Taking a pawn with a piece that has already moved twice can be expensive, as the exercises below show.

💡 Quick checklist for the first 10 moves: 1 or 2 center pawns · knights · bishops · castle · connected rooks. If a move doesn't help any of these or answer a threat, be suspicious.

Principles aren't laws

All of these rules have exceptions. If your opponent leaves a piece hanging, take it — even if it "delays" your development — as long as you've checked there's no trap. And answering a threat always comes before the checklist. To see the principles applied to real openings, read chess openings for beginners.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best first move in chess?

There isn't a single best one. 1.e4 and 1.d4 are the most popular and follow the principles: they take the center and free your pieces. 1.c4 and 1.Nf3 are excellent too.

How many moves does the opening last?

There's no fixed number, but usually the first 10 to 15 moves. In practice, the opening is over when both sides have developed their pieces and made their kings safe.

Why shouldn't I bring my queen out early?

Because she becomes a target. Your opponent attacks her while developing pieces, and you lose time retreating.

Should I always castle?

In the vast majority of games, yes, and preferably early. There are exceptions, mainly when the queens have been traded and the king becomes useful in the center.

Do I need to memorize openings to get started?

No. By following the principles you'll play sensible openings without memorizing anything. Memorizing makes more sense later, to go deeper into the openings you enjoy playing.
