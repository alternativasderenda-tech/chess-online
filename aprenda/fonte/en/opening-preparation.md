Opening preparation means building and studying a **repertoire**: a small, consistent set of lines you always play, with a ready answer to each of your opponent's main moves. A good repertoire starts with **ideas** — typical pawn structures and plans — and only then goes down to the moves; it lives in notes you can review; and it keeps being corrected with your own games. The goal is not to memorize as much theory as possible but to reach a middlegame you understand.

## What a repertoire is {#repertorio}

A tournament player's minimum repertoire has three parts:

1. **As White:** a first move (1.e4, 1.d4 or something else) and answers to the main defenses.
2. **As Black against 1.e4:** one defense.
3. **As Black against 1.d4:** one defense, which also needs a plan against 1.c4 and 1.Nf3.

Consistency helps a lot. If your openings lead to similar structures — say, closed pawn chains, or positions with an isolated pawn — the middlegame plans repeat and what you learn in one carries over to the other. If you're still choosing, start with the options in [chess openings for beginners](artigo:aberturas-para-iniciantes) and with your style: open, tactical positions, or closed, maneuvering ones.

## Ideas before moves {#ideias}

Memorizing a sequence without understanding [why](artigo:principios-de-abertura) works until your opponent leaves the book — and they always do. For each opening in your repertoire, first answer:

- **What is the typical pawn structure?** It defines the plans (see [pawn structure](artigo:estrutura-de-peoes)).
- **Where do the pieces go?** The typical squares for each knight and bishop.
- **What are the pawn breaks?** The pawn advances that free the position, for you and for your opponent.
- **What kind of endgame tends to arise?** If the opening often leads to endgames where you're worse, better to know it early.

```tabuleiro
fen: rn1qkbnr/pp2pppp/2p5/5b2/3PN3/8/PPP2PPP/R1BQKBNR w KQkq - 1 5
casas: f5 c6 e4:amarela
legenda: Caro-Kann Defense after 1.e4 c6 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5. Black has developed the light-squared bishop before closing the chain with ...e6 — an idea, not just a move.
```

In this [Caro-Kann](artigo:francesa-e-caro-kann) example, understanding that the c8 bishop comes out **before** ...e6 is worth more than memorizing ten moves: that's why the move exists, and that's what you'll remember when your opponent deviates from the main line.

## How deep to go into theory {#profundidade}

The right depth depends on your level and the type of opening:

- **Positional openings** (systems like the [London](artigo:gambito-da-dama-e-london#london), closed structures): few concrete moves, many ideas. You can stay shallow.
- **Tactical openings** (gambits, sharp [Sicilian](artigo:defesa-siciliana) lines): a mistake in the first moves can lose the game. Here you need to know the critical lines precisely.
- **Priority:** study first the lines you actually meet in your games, not the ones that fill the books.

:::dica
**💡 Tip:** a good rule is to study each line until the position "becomes a familiar middlegame" — when you recognize the structure and know the plan. Past that point, understanding beats memorizing.
:::

## Move orders and transpositions {#transposicoes}

The same position can arise along different paths. That's called a **transposition**, and it is both a weapon and a trap.

```tabuleiro
fen: rnbqkb1r/ppp2ppp/4pn2/3p4/2PP4/2N5/PP2PPPP/R1BQKBNR w KQkq - 2 4
casas: c4 d4 d5 e6
legenda: Queen's Gambit Declined. This position arises both from 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 d5 and from 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e6 3.Nc3 Nf6.
```

Knowing your transpositions lets you:

- **Avoid lines you don't want**, by choosing the move order that takes options away from your opponent.
- **Reuse your study:** if two move orders reach the same position, you study it once.
- **Stay calm** when your opponent reaches a known position by an unusual route.

## Traps: know them so you don't fall in, and so you can punish {#armadilhas}

Every opening has traps, and your repertoire should include the ones in your lines — both to avoid them and to cash in when your opponent falls for them. A classic example is **Légal's mate**: after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.Bc4 Bg4 4.Nc3 g6?, White plays 5.Nxe5!, apparently giving up the queen. If 5...Bxd1??, mate in two follows (you solved it in [opening principles](artigo:principios-de-abertura)). Instead, Black should accept losing a pawn — for example, 5...dxe5 6.Qxg4.

```tabuleiro
fen: rn1qkbnr/ppp2p1p/3p2p1/4p3/2B1P1b1/2N2N2/PPPP1PPP/R1BQK2R w KQkq - 0 5
setas: f3e5:vermelha g4d1:azul
legenda: Before Légal's mate: 5.Nxe5! leaves the white queen "hanging" on d1, but taking it costs the game.
```

## Organize and review {#organize}

A repertoire only works if it's organized and up to date:

1. **Keep one file per opening:** the tree of main moves, the typical structure, both sides' plans, the traps and one or two annotated model games.
2. **After every game, compare it with the file.** Where did the game leave your repertoire? Who left it, you or your opponent? What was the right move? Write it down.
3. **Before tournaments, review** only the lines you play, not everything you've ever studied.
4. **Practice the positions** by playing against the computer and in faster games, so the plans come naturally.

## Computers and databases: in moderation {#computador}

Engines and game databases are excellent tools for opening preparation, but they're easy to misuse:

- **Don't copy the evaluation; understand the plan.** A move the engine approves of can lead to positions you don't know how to play. Prefer lines whose ideas you can explain.
- **Use master games** to see how the plans work in practice: where the pieces go, which pawn breaks happen, which endgames arise.
- **Check your own games.** They show the lines you actually face, at your level, rather than the ones in fashion among professionals.

:::pratique Test your repertoire
Play 5 games against the computer with the same opening and compare each one with your file: where did the game leave what you studied?
:::

## Exercises

Both exercises are traps you haven't met yet in this course. Notice that both depend on move order: a single move played at the wrong moment (4.e3? in the Albin, 5.Nxe5? in the Ruy Lopez) is enough.

```exercicio
fen: rnbqk1nr/ppp2ppp/8/4P3/1BP5/4p3/PP3PPP/RN1QKBNR b KQkq - 0 6
enunciado: Albin Countergambit: 1.d4 d5 2.c4 e5 3.dxe5 d4 4.e3? Bb4+ 5.Bd2 dxe3! 6.Bxb4??. Black to play and win decisive material.
tipo: tatica
solucao: e3f2 e1e2 f2g1n
dica: Start with a pawn check. When you promote, a queen is not the best choice.
explicacao: 6...exf2+ 7.Ke2 (7.Kxf2? Qxd1) fxg1=N+! — promoting to a knight gives check. If 8.Rxg1, 8...Bg4+ wins the queen; if 8.Ke1, 8...Qh4+ and Black, a piece up, attacks the king (8.Kf2 or 8.Ke3? Qxd1). Promoting to a queen (7...fxg1=Q?) would allow 8.Qxd8+. This is the Lasker Trap, a must-know for anyone who plays the Albin.
```

```exercicio
fen: r1bqkb1r/pp1pnppp/2p2n2/4N3/2B1P3/3P4/PPP2PPP/RNBQK2R b KQkq - 1 6
enunciado: Ruy Lopez, Mortimer Trap: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5 Nf6 4.d3 Ne7 5.Nxe5? c6 6.Bc4?. Black to play and **win a piece**.
solucao: d8a5
dica: The e5 knight has been left undefended. A check could hit it at the same time.
explicacao: 6...Qa5+! checks along the a5-e1 diagonal and hits the e5 knight along the fifth rank. Whatever White does about the check (c3, b4, Nc3, Nd2, Bd2, Qd2 or a king move), the knight falls: 7...Qxe5. Anyone who plays the Ruy Lopez as White needs to know that 5.Nxe5 is a mistake in this move order.
```

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### How many openings do I need to know?
One as White and two as Black (one against 1.e4 and one against 1.d4) already make a complete tournament repertoire.

### Should I memorize lines?
Understand the ideas first: pawn structure, piece placement and pawn breaks. Then memorize the critical lines, especially in tactical openings.

### How far should I study each line?
Until the position becomes a middlegame you recognize and know how to play. Beyond that, prioritize the lines that come up in your own games.

### How do I review my repertoire?
After each game, find where it left your repertoire and what the right move would have been, and add it to that opening's file.
