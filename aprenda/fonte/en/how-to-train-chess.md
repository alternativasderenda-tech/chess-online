To improve at chess, combine five activities every week in balanced proportions: **tactics** (the biggest share, because they decide most games), **endgames**, **openings**, **games played** with time to think, and **analysis of your own games**. What makes the difference is not the number of hours but regularity and feedback: play, find out where you went wrong, and train exactly that. A plan of 30 to 60 minutes a day, followed for months, beats occasional marathons.

## The five pillars of training {#pilares}

1. **Tactics.** Solving exercises every day trains [pattern recognition](artigo:taticas#padroes): forks, pins, typical mates, sacrifices. The more patterns you know, the less you have to calculate from scratch.
2. **Endgames.** A handful of theoretical endgames covers a huge part of practical play: opposition, Lucena, Philidor, the [wrong-colored bishop](artigo:bispos#faq). Studying endgames also sharpens your technique and your feel for piece activity.
3. **Openings.** Just enough to reach a middlegame you understand — ideas and structures before moves.
4. **Games played.** Training without playing never turns into practice. Prefer time controls that give you time to think.
5. **Analysis.** This is where learning happens: every game shows exactly what you need to train.

## A weekly plan {#plano}

This plan assumes about 45 minutes a day. Adjust the proportions to your schedule, but keep the order of priorities.

| Day | Main activity | Details |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monday | Tactics | Exercises from the Learn section, solved without moving the pieces |
| Tuesday | Endgames | One theoretical position a week, played until it's automatic |
| Wednesday | Game against the AI + analysis | One game with time to think, then a move-by-move review |
| Thursday | Openings | Review your repertoire file and the week's games |
| Friday | Tactics and calculation | Mate-in-3 or mate-in-4 exercises, calculating the whole line |
| Saturday | Tournament | Games against people or an AI ranking |
| Sunday | Analysis and rest | Review the tournament games and log your mistakes |

:::dica
**💡 Tip:** keep a mistake log. For every game you analyze, write down the type of mistake (tactical, calculation, opening, endgame, clock). After a month, the log tells you what to train.
:::

## Tactics: every day, but the right way {#taticas}

The wrong way to train tactics is to click the first move that looks good. The right way is to treat every exercise like a position from a real game:

- **Calculate the whole line** before moving: your move, the best defense and the follow-up. (See [calculation and visualization](artigo:calculo).)
- **Start with forcing moves:** checks, captures and threats.
- **When you get one wrong, understand the pattern**, not just the answer. The same pattern will come back in another position.

The exercises in each Learn article are built for this: they're organized by theme, and every solution has been checked — with the game's engine and, for endgames, with the endgame tablebase.

```tabuleiro
fen: r5rk/pp1b1Npp/2pq4/8/8/2P5/PP3PPP/R4RK1 b - - 1 2
casas: h8:vermelha g8 g7 h7
legenda: Smothered mate: a pattern that, once you've seen it a few times, you recognize in seconds. Training patterns is what turns calculation into recognition.
```

## Endgames: few, but well known {#finais}

You don't need to study hundreds of endgames. Start with the ones that come up most and play each against the computer until you can execute it without thinking:

1. [King and pawn versus king](artigo:finais-de-peao): the **opposition**.
2. Rook and pawn versus rook: **Lucena** and **Philidor** (see [rook endgames](artigo:finais-de-torre)).
3. Bishop and rook pawn of the wrong color.
4. [Basic mates](artigo:mates-basicos) with queen and with rook.

```tabuleiro
fen: 3K4/3P1k2/8/8/8/8/2r5/4R3 w - - 0 1
setas: e1f1 f1f4
legenda: The Lucena position: one of the few theoretical positions every tournament player must know by heart.
```

## Games against the AI and tournaments {#partidas}

Play against the site's AI at **increasing difficulty**: start at a level where you win most games, and move up once you win comfortably. The AI never gets tired or distracted, so it punishes tactical carelessness — exactly what you want to train.

Two rules make those games useful:

- **Play with time to think** and apply your routine on every move: opponent's threats, candidates, calculation, final check.
- **[Analyze every game you lose](artigo:analisar-partidas)**, and the wins that came with a scare. Look for the first move where the evaluation changed.

When you're ready for human opponents, the [site's tournaments](/en/tournaments) offer knockouts against other people and rankings of games against the AI. Tournaments give you something training can't: pressure to get a result. That pressure is what shows whether your routine works.

```tabuleiro
fen: r1bqk1nr/pppp1ppp/2n5/2b1p3/2B1P3/5N2/PPPP1PPP/RNBQK2R w KQkq - 4 4
legenda: Italian Game (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Bc5): a good opening to practice against the AI, with clear plans for both sides.
```

To choose your openings, see [chess openings for beginners](artigo:aberturas-para-iniciantes) and, to organize them, [opening preparation](artigo:preparacao-de-aberturas).

## How to analyze your own games {#analise}

Use the [6-step method](artigo:analisar-partidas#metodo) and, at the end, turn the mistake into training: lots of endgame mistakes? More endgames on Tuesday. Lots of moves made in time trouble? Work on [time management](artigo:relogio-e-psicologia). Write every mistake in your log, with its type.

## How to measure progress {#progresso}

Ratings go up and down for many reasons, and bad weeks happen to everyone. To know whether your training is working, also track more direct signals:

- **Serious mistakes per game.** Count the moves that lost material or the game. That number should go down over time.
- **Accuracy on exercises.** Getting the whole line right, against the best defense, matters more than speed.
- **Endgames played without doubt.** If you play the Lucena or the opposition without having to think, that topic is learned.
- **AI level.** Moving up a difficulty level and keeping good results is a concrete measure of progress.

Review the plan every month, with your mistake log in hand, and change the proportions of your training according to what it shows.

## Exercises

```exercicio
fen: R7/5p2/6p1/3k3p/7P/6P1/5P1K/3q4 w - - 0 1
enunciado: Tactic of the day. Black has a queen against a rook, but the king and queen are on the same file. White to play and win.
solucao: a8d8 d5c4 d8d1
dica: A check on the d-file moves the king out of the way.
explicacao: 1.Rd8+! is a skewer: the black king has to leave the d-file and 2.Rxd1 wins the queen. Every king move allows the capture.
```

```exercicio
fen: 7K/8/k1P5/7p/8/8/8/8 w - - 0 1
enunciado: Endgame of the day: Richard Réti's famous study (1921). The white king looks hopelessly far away. White to play and draw.
solucao: h8g7
dica: The king can chase the h-pawn and, at the same time, approach its own pawn.
explicacao: 1.Kg7! h4 2.Kf6, and now 2...Kb6 3.Ke5! catches the h-pawn, while 2...h3 3.Ke7 h2 4.c7 Kb7 5.Kd7 also draws, because both pawns promote. The king walks down the diagonal and serves two goals at once. According to the endgame tablebase, it's the only drawing move.
```

## Frequently asked questions {#faq}

### How much time a day should I train chess?
Thirty to sixty minutes a day, done regularly, already brings visible progress. Consistency beats long, rare sessions.

### What should I train first: tactics, openings or endgames?
Tactics, because they decide most games. Then basic endgames, and openings only as much as you need to reach a good middlegame.

### Does playing lots of fast games help?
It builds practice, but without analysis you learn little. Prefer fewer, longer games, and analyze all of them.

### How do I use Chess Online to train?
Solve the exercises in the Learn section, play the AI while gradually raising the difficulty, and join the site's tournaments.
